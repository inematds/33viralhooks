What a hook is (and why it determines the video’s fate)

One hook it's the bait in the first few seconds of a video: the first line + the first image that appear before any explanation. It has one mission — to make the person not swipe to the next one.

Notice that a “hook” isn’t an “introduction.” An introduction sets up the topic; a hook grabs your attention. In a short video, there’s no time to set anything up: either you hook viewers in seconds, or you’ve lost them.

🆕 New here? What is “scrolling”?

"Scroll" is the act of swiping through the feed with your finger (TikTok, Reels, Shorts). "Scroll stop"—stopping the scroll—is the goal of the hook: the scene that makes your thumb stop on your video instead of moving on to the next one.

🧠 Why the first 3 seconds are worth more than the rest

On short-form video platforms, people decide in ~1 to 3 seconds whether to keep watching. If the hook fails, they swipe away and the algorithm understands that your video "doesn't hold attention"—and stops showing it.

  • •The rest of the script only gets watched if the hook holds their attention.
  • •A good video with a weak hook dies; an average video with a strong hook goes viral.
  • •That's why professional creators spend more time on the hook than on everything else combined.

🎬 Visual: empty office chair, screen with the warning "Position Closed"

🔤 Text: AI Took the Job

All 3 layers point to the same threat in the first frames—and the line still opens a gap ("so what’s shocking?").

Retention, watch time, and CTR — the metrics the hook affects

The hook isn’t aesthetics: it drives numbers that the algorithm uses to decide whether to show your content to more people. It’s worth defining each one because the entire course revolves around them.

🆕 New here? The 3 terms, one sentence each

Retention = what percentage of the video a person watches before leaving. Watch-time = the total amount of time people spend watching. CTR (click/swipe-through rate) = what percentage of people who saw the video actually stopped to watch instead of scrolling.

▸ Going deeper: the retention curve

Every platform shows a retention graph: the horizontal axis is the video’s duration, and the vertical axis is how many % are still watching. The steepest drop almost always happens in the first 3 seconds — that's where a weak hook loses the audience. A strong hook “flattens” that initial drop and keeps the curve high, the #1 signal the algorithm looks for.

💡 The “first frame” rule

The first frame (the still image that appears before playback) is already part of the hook. If it’s generic, the person scrolls past before the audio starts. That’s why, in this course, every hook has a VISUAL layer.

3

Pattern interrupt: why the brain stops at the unexpected

O pattern interrupt (pattern interrupt) is the psychological mechanism behind every hook that works: the brain ignores the expected and wakes up to the unexpected.

🆕 New here? What is a “pattern interrupt”

It's anything that breaks expectations: an outrageous claim, a strange image, an overly specific number. The feed is a predictable sequence; whatever breaks the pattern gets automatic attention — before the person even decides to pay attention.

✓ Breaks the pattern (stops the thumb)

  • ✓A statement that goes against common sense ("it’s not about speed")
  • ✓Unexpected image (empty office chair + "Position Closed")
  • ✓An overly specific number (“did it in 4 seconds”)

✗ Confirms the pattern (the thumb scrolls)

  • ✗Generic opening ("Hey, everyone, today I’m going to talk about…")
  • ✗Cliché image with no tension
  • ✗Vague promise ("incredible AI tips")

🧠 3 pattern-breaking levers

null

  • •Plausible absurdity: something that seems impossible but could be real ("this robot learned to lie").
  • •Too specific: a number no one would make up (“US$500 in US$15.000”).
  • •Taboo / prohibited: "convinced a human to break the law," "what no one noticed."

💡 Specific beats generic

"I made money" doesn’t stop anyone. "I turned $500 into $15,000 without touching the stock market" does—because specifics create a concrete image and a question ("how?").

🧩 Quick check (optional, doesn't block progress)

You have 2 versions of the same opening line. Which one stops the scroll more?

The anatomy: hook + tension + promise

Every strong hook has three internal gears. Recognizing them makes you take apart and rebuild any viral hook.

🧠 The 3 gears

null

  • •Hook (attention): the pattern break that stops the scroll — the shocking or strange part.
  • •Tension (gap): the question left hanging that demands an answer ("but that’s not what’s shocking…").
  • •Promise (reward): the implied promise that it’s worth staying — you’ll discover something useful or surprising.

🔬 Breaking down “Most people fear AI… I fear the people controlling it.”

💬 Spoken: "Most people fear AI… I fear who controls it."

🎬 Visual: silhouette controlling a network of robots

🔤 Text: Who Controls AI

Hook: recognizable fear. Tension: the twist ("I’m afraid of something ELSE"). Promise: a more sophisticated view of the topic.

⚠️ Missing a gear? The hook falls flat

Hook alone = shocking, then empty. Promise alone = sounds vague. Tension is the glue that holds everything together between the shock and the payoff.

5

Why the algorithm rewards hooks

The algorithm doesn’t “like” your video—it measures reactions and delivers what captures attention. The hook is what triggers the right reactions at the start.

🧠 The delivery cycle

null

  • •You publish → the platform shows it to a small batch of people.
  • •If the hook holds attention (high retention in the first 3 seconds, few “skips”), it understands “this keeps people watching” and expands the batch.
  • •More reach → more signals → more distribution. The hook triggers the first snowball.

📊 What the platform looks for in the first few seconds

  • •How many swiped away before 3s (the fewer, the better).
  • •The slope of the retention curve right at the beginning.
  • •Re-watches and profile visits — signs that the opening worked.
  • •Comments/shares the hook prompted.
▸ Going deeper: the "batch test"

Think of each video as going through a series of “batch tests.” Batch 1 is ~200-500 people. If retention and engagement hit a certain threshold, it moves to batch 2 (a few thousand), then batch 3, and so on. The hook decides whether you make it past batch 1—which is why two videos with the same content and different hooks can have completely opposite outcomes.

💡 A good hook can't save bad content — but good content dies with a bad hook

Think of the hook as the gatekeeper: it decides who gets in. Without it, no one reaches your best content.

6

Myths that hold beginner creators back

Before you get into practice, it’s worth debunking the beliefs that make beginners waste good videos on weak hooks.

✓ Truth

  • ✓The hook is the most important part—it deserves more time.
  • ✓You can (and should) reuse hook formulas that have already proven effective.
  • ✓The visual and on-screen text are part of the hook, not just the spoken line.

✗ Myth

  • ✗"If the content is good, it finds its audience on its own."
  • ✗"Using a formula is cheating / isn’t authentic."
  • ✗"A hook is just the first spoken sentence."
Common beliefReality

"I have to show up on camera / speak well"Visuals + text hold attention even when you’re not on screen
"It’s luck / it went viral out of nowhere"Repeatable pattern: hook → retention → delivery
